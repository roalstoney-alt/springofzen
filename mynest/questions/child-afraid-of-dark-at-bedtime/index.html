OWN-ROOM TRANSITION · AGES 2½–6

What Should I Do When My Child Is Afraid of the Dark at Bedtime?

Short answer

Fear of darkness can appear even after months of easy sleep. Before adding several lights or changing the entire routine, check whether the fear starts at lights-out, after a shadow appears, or only when the parent leaves. That difference tells you whether light is the best first experiment or whether separation is the stronger barrier.

What to check first

  • Does fear begin exactly when the light changes?
  • Is a particular shadow or reflection involved?
  • Would the child be calm in the same darkness with a parent present?
  • Is the fear present in other rooms during daytime?
  • Are persistent nightmares or severe distress involved?

Possible barriers

The labels below are observation categories, not diagnoses.

  • Darkness or shadowsF01
  • Being alone rather than darkness itselfF02
  • Unfamiliar room cuesF03
  • Glare, noise, or temperatureF04
  • Changing responses each nightF05
  • Persistent nightmares or another causeF06

What not to change yet

Do not redesign the entire room or change the bed, lighting, routine, and comfort objects at the same time. Do not buy several products before you know where resistance begins.

One first experiment

Offer one approved light choice—a low warm light, soft moon light, or low bedside glow. Keep brightness, placement, colour temperature, flashing, electrical safety, and usage period under adult control.

When room changes may not be enough

Room changes are not a substitute for qualified care. Seek appropriate professional advice for pain, breathing problems, persistent nightmares, major life change, severe distress, safety concerns, sleepwalking, or anything that feels medically unusual.
